Guides and Protocols

Our laboratory uses standardized protocols for the collection, preservation, extraction, and quantification of nucleic acids, specifically optimized for water hyacinth (Pontederia crassipes).

Two distinct sampling strategies are used according to the biogeographic status of the population: (1) populations occurring in invaded areas and (2) populations occurring within the native range of P. crassipes. These scenarios differ primarily in the spatial scale at which sampling is designed.

🌿 1. Tissue Collection and Herborization

Sampling Design

Sampling in Invaded Areas

In invaded regions, P. crassipes populations commonly occur in relatively delimited inland freshwater systems such as lakes, lagoons, wetlands, reservoirs, ponds, and other enclosed or semi-enclosed water bodies.

For each sampling site or population:

  1. Collect samples from three individual plants.
  2. Select the three individuals as far apart from one another as the site and accessibility allow.
  3. Because P. crassipes reproduces extensively through clonal propagation, avoid collecting visibly interconnected ramets or individuals belonging to the same compact clonal mat whenever possible.
  4. From each individual, reserve approximately 3–5 healthy leaves, depending on tissue availability.
  5. Keep the tissue collected from each individual separate and assign a unique sample code to each individual.
  6. If site size or accessibility prevents wide separation among individuals, maximize the feasible distance among samples and document this limitation under fieldNotes.

The objective is to obtain three spatially separated individuals that are representative of the sampled population while minimizing, as far as possible, repeated sampling of the same clone.

Sampling in the Native Range

Within the native range of P. crassipes, particularly in rivers, floodplains, and connected aquatic systems of the Amazon and other South American hydrological basins, sampling should be designed to represent the basin or connected hydrological system, rather than a single isolated locality.

This basin-level sampling framework is consistent with previous population genomic sampling of water hyacinth across hydrological basins within its native range (da Cunha et al., 2022).

For native-range sampling:

  1. Sampling should be distributed among multiple points along the river, basin, or connected hydrological system, whenever access and logistical conditions permit.
  2. Where a clear flow direction or longitudinal hydrological gradient exists, sampling points should be distributed, whenever feasible, among upstream, intermediate, and downstream portions of the system. This is an operational criterion adopted by the initiative to maximize spatial representation of the basin.
  3. Where no clear upstream–downstream gradient is present, sampling points should instead be distributed as broadly as possible throughout the accessible portion of the basin or aquatic system.
  4. At each sampling point, collect samples from three individual plants.
  5. The three individuals at each point should be as spatially separated as local conditions allow, avoiding visibly interconnected ramets whenever possible.
  6. From each individual, reserve approximately 3–5 healthy leaves, depending on tissue availability.
  7. Keep each individual as an independent sample and assign it a unique sample code.
  8. Sampling should continue at multiple points along the basin whenever feasible, with the objective of obtaining representative spatial coverage of the hydrological system.
  9. The relative position of each sampling point within the basin or river system should be documented under fieldNotes.

Therefore, in the native range, three individuals correspond to each sampling point and not to the entire basin.

Tissue Collection for DNA Extraction

For optimal DNA preservation during field sampling and subsequent transport or storage, tissue should be dried using silica gel immediately after collection.

  1. Select healthy, preferably young leaves of P. crassipes.
  2. Reserve approximately 3–5 leaves from each sampled individual, according to tissue availability.
  3. Cut representative portions of the leaves into smaller pieces, avoiding excessively thick, highly hydrated, or spongy tissues whenever possible.
  4. Place tissue from each individual in a separate, clearly labeled zip-lock bag containing color-indicating silica gel.
  5. Do not mix tissue from different individuals in the same bag.
  6. Ensure that sufficient silica gel surrounds the plant material to promote rapid dehydration.
  7. Allow the tissue to dry completely. Properly dried tissue should become brittle and snap when bent.
  8. Replace or regenerate silica gel when the color indicator shows excessive moisture.

Fresh material may also be processed when DNA extraction can be performed locally shortly after collection and an appropriate cold chain is available. However, silica-gel drying is the standard preservation method recommended for samples that will be transported or stored before DNA extraction.

Mandatory Photographic Documentation

Photographic documentation is mandatory for every sampling site or sampling point.

Collaborators must archive, at minimum:

  1. A general photograph of the sampled population and sampling site, showing the water body and surrounding environmental context.
  2. A photograph of representative P. crassipes individuals in situ, preferably showing diagnostic vegetative and reproductive structures when available.
  3. A photograph of the voucher or pressed specimen associated with the collection.

Additional photographs documenting population density, associated vegetation, habitat characteristics, flowers, fruits, environmental disturbances, or other relevant features are strongly encouraged.

Photographs must be associated with the corresponding locality and sample codes so that they can be linked unambiguously to the global sample database.

Herborization and Voucher Specimens

A voucher specimen of each collected plant must be deposited in a recognized local herbarium. Additionally, high-quality photographs of the population or sampling site, the plant in its habitat, and the pressed specimen must be archived by the collaborator.

Follow the specific herbarium protocols of your local institution. If no specific guidelines for aquatic plants are provided by your local herbarium, use the following method adapted for aquatic flora (Rodríguez & Rojas, 2006):

  • Aquatic Plant Pressing: Do not press directly into dry newspaper, as delicate aquatic parts (like Pontederia flowers) may adhere to the paper and be destroyed upon removal.
  • Collect the plants in wet newspaper and roll them up.
  • In the laboratory/herbarium, place the plant in a tray of water to allow all parts to spread out and extend naturally.
  • Slide a piece of white cardstock under the plant and carefully lift it out of the water, keeping the plant fixed to the cardstock.
  • Place the cardstock with the plant inside the pressing paper (newspaper/blotters) and proceed with standard drying methods.

Mandatory Sample Metadata

Regardless of the specific label requirements of the local herbarium for the voucher specimen, all collaborators must record and retain the following metadata for every collected sample. This information is critical for our global database.

For samples from the native range, the relative location of the sampling point within the hydrological system, such as upstream, intermediate, downstream, or another relevant spatial description, should also be recorded under fieldNotes.

Please record the data using these exact fields:

🧬 2. DNA Extraction

DNeasy® Plant Pro Kit (QIAGEN)

The DNeasy® Plant Pro Kit (QIAGEN) is the DNA extraction kit used, tested, and validated in our laboratory for Pontederia crassipes. The protocol described below corresponds to the workflow currently used by our laboratory.

The reagent conditions indicated below follow the manufacturer’s recommendations for plant tissues rich in phenolic compounds and other secondary metabolites. They should therefore not be interpreted as an independent modification developed by the initiative.

While the specific brand or model of tissue disruption equipment may vary among collaborating laboratories, adequate tissue pulverization is essential for obtaining high-quality DNA.

Starting Material & Pre-treatment

  • Silica-gel dried leaf tissue is the standard starting material for samples collected, transported, or stored before extraction.
  • Fresh or recently collected tissue may also be processed when adequate cold storage and rapid extraction are available.
  • Crucial step: immediately before disruption, freeze the tissue using liquid nitrogen. If liquid nitrogen is unavailable, place the tissue in a -20°C or -80°C freezer overnight, or follow an equivalent validated procedure for maintaining the tissue frozen before homogenization.

Preparation and Homogenization

  1. Transfer the frozen tissue to the appropriate disruption tube.
  2. Lysis Buffer: Add 5–100 mg of tissue to a 2 ml tissue disruption tube.
  3. Following the DNeasy® Plant Pro Kit recommendations for plant material with high phenolic content, add 450 µl of Solution CD1 and 50 µl of Solution PS.
  4. Vortex briefly to mix.
  5. Homogenization: Any suitable tissue disruption system available in the collaborating laboratory may be used, including a vortex with an appropriate adapter, bead beater, or tissue homogenizer.
  6. Ensure complete tissue disruption. In our workflow, samples are vortexed at the maximum operational setting for approximately 3–5 minutes.

Extraction Steps

  1. Centrifuge the disruption tubes at 12,000 x g for 2 min.
  2. Transfer the supernatant to a clean 1.5 ml tube.
  3. Add 200 µl of Solution CD2 and vortex for 5 s.
  4. Centrifuge at 12,000 x g for 1 min.
  5. Avoiding the pellet, transfer the supernatant to a clean 1.5 ml tube.
  6. Add 500 µl of Buffer APP and vortex for 5 s.
  7. Load 600 µl of lysate onto an MB Spin Column.
  8. Centrifuge at 12,000 x g for 1 min and discard the flow-through. Repeat if necessary to process the entire lysate volume.
  9. Place the column into a clean 2 ml collection tube.
  10. Add 650 µl of Buffer AW1, centrifuge at 12,000 x g for 1 min, and discard the flow-through.
  11. Add 650 µl of Buffer AW2, centrifuge at 12,000 x g for 1 min, and discard the flow-through.
  12. Centrifuge the column at up to 16,000 x g for 2 min to completely dry the membrane.
  13. Elution: Add 50–100 µl of Buffer EB directly to the center of the membrane, allow it to sit briefly at room temperature, and centrifuge at 12,000 x g for 1 min.

Mandatory RNase Treatment

The genomic DNA must undergo RNase treatment as part of the extraction or post-extraction cleanup workflow because RNase-treated DNA is required by the sequencing provider used by the initiative.

  • Use RNase A or another DNase-free RNase compatible with genomic DNA purification.
  • The amount of enzyme, incubation temperature, and incubation time should follow the instructions of the RNase product used by the collaborating laboratory.
  • If RNase treatment is performed after DNA elution, perform any subsequent cleanup required by the RNase protocol or sequencing provider.
  • Do not omit RNase treatment from samples intended for genomic sequencing.

Because RNase formulations differ among manufacturers, a universal RNase volume is not specified here.

💧 3. DNA Quantification & Quality Control

Collaborators may use any spectrophotometer or fluorometer available in their facilities. However, because P. crassipes contains substantial quantities of secondary metabolites, spectrophotometric and fluorometric measurements should be interpreted differently.

Quality Assessment: Spectrophotometry (e.g., NanoDrop)

Spectrophotometers measure absorbance and may be affected by phytochemicals, carbohydrates, phenols, RNA, and other contaminants commonly encountered in plant DNA extractions. Therefore, this method should be used primarily to assess DNA purity and quality rather than absolute DNA concentration.

  • A260/280 Ratio: Values of approximately 1.8–2.0 are generally expected for relatively pure DNA.
  • A260/230 Ratio: An ideal range is approximately 1.8–2.2. In plant samples containing high concentrations of secondary metabolites, this ratio may be lower; however, values substantially below 1.5 may indicate contamination capable of affecting downstream PCR or NGS applications (Desjardins et al., 2009).

Quantity Assessment: Fluorometry (e.g., Qubit, DeNovix FX)

Accurate DNA concentration must be determined using a fluorometric method.

Fluorometric assays use dyes with greater specificity for double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) and are therefore less affected by the absorbance interference caused by plant secondary metabolites.

  • Always report the final DNA concentration in ng/µl based on fluorometric measurements.
  • Fluorometric concentration should be used to calculate the amount of DNA available for genomic sequencing.

Approximate DNA Concentrations Observed in Our Laboratory

The following concentrations are provided only as approximate empirical reference values obtained during our work with P. crassipes. They are not minimum acceptance thresholds and should not be interpreted as guaranteed extraction yields.

  • Fresh or recently collected material: approximately 20–30 ng/µl.
  • Material stored for longer periods in silica gel: approximately 4–8 ng/µl.

Lower concentrations may also occur. DNA recovery depends on multiple factors, including tissue condition, storage duration, silica-gel efficiency, initial tissue quality, tissue quantity, homogenization efficiency, and extraction performance.

Consequently, each DNA sample must be quantified individually by fluorometry rather than assuming its concentration from tissue condition or preservation time.

🧬 4. Genomic Sequencing Guidelines

Collaborators may use any sequencing facility available to them and should follow the specific sample-submission requirements established by their selected sequencing provider.

Whenever possible, sequencing depth should be maximized within the limits of the project objectives, sequencing strategy, available budget, and laboratory resources.

If a sequencing facility does not provide more specific submission guidelines, the following parameters may be used as baseline reference requirements:

  • Total Amount & Quality: At least 100 ng of high molecular weight (>20 kb) genomic DNA.
  • RNase Treatment: DNA submitted for genomic sequencing must have undergone RNase treatment.
  • Target Concentration: Approximately 5.0–10.0 ng/µl is recommended for submission. Depending on the sequencing provider, acceptable concentrations may range approximately from 0.5 ng/µl to 25 ng/µl.
  • Quantification Method: Samples must be quantified using Qubit, PicoGreen, DeNovix FX, or another dsDNA-specific fluorometric assay.
  • Purity Assessment: Spectrophotometric measurements should be used to evaluate DNA purity and potential contamination but should not replace fluorometric quantification.
  • Final QC: Before shipment or sequencing submission, verify DNA concentration, purity, integrity when possible, completion of RNase treatment, and compliance with all additional requirements established by the selected sequencing facility.

🧬 5. Spatial Sampling Strategies

The sampling framework used by the initiative follows two clearly differentiated spatial strategies:

Invaded areas: sampling site or water body → 3 spatially separated individuals → approximately 3–5 leaves per individual.

Native range: basin or connected hydrological system → multiple sampling points distributed along the system → 3 spatially separated individuals per sampling point → approximately 3–5 leaves per individual.

The basin-level sampling approach for populations within the native range is consistent with previous population genomic sampling of P. crassipes across South American hydrological basins (da Cunha et al., 2022).

Reference

da Cunha, N. L., Xue, H., Wright, S. I., & Barrett, S. C. H. (2022). Genetic variation and clonal diversity in floating aquatic plants: Comparative genomic analysis of water hyacinth species in their native range. Molecular Ecology, 31, 5307–5325. https://doi.org/10.1111/mec.16664
